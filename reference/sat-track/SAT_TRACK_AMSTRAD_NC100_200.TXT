Program Description:

The final program is a  single-satellite tracker for the
Amstrad NC100/NC200 portable computers written in BBC BASIC
By Alvin Banderas

It simulates a 24-hour pass window based on user-supplied 
initial conditions and observer location. 

For each visible pass (satellite above the geometric horizon
and in favourable lighting),it displays two screens:

Screen 1:Pass number, satellite name, current time, 
and the satellite’s Cartesian coordinates (X, Y, Z in km).  

Screen 2:Pass number, observation period, local time, 
compass bearing (degrees clockwise from north), elevation, 
visibility (GOOD/POOR),Altitude (km),Velocity (m/s) 
and acceleration (m/s²).

A beep (VDU7) sounds when a new pass is about to be shown, 
and a pass counter keeps track of how many passes have been 
displayed.At the end,the total number of passes is printed.

Key Features:

1/ User-defined satellite:
 
Name, initial position (km) and 
velocity (km/s) are entered manually.

Date and time: 

2/ Full date (with leap-year handling) 
and start hour/minute are validated.

3/ Observer location:

longitude and latitude are requested and validated.

4/ Numerical integration:

5/ Five-step iteration over 15-minute intervals propagates 
the orbit using a simple Euler-type method.

6/ Visibility check:-satellite is considered visible 
if it is above the geometric horizon and not in the middle
of the day (crude 06:06-17:54 daylight exclusion).

7/ Local coordinate transformation: 
up, north & east vectors are derived from the observer’s 
position to compute azimuth and elevation.

8/ Formatted output: 
numbers are displayed with the correct
decimal places using the `@%` system variable; screens 
are cleared between passes.

9/ Pass counter: 
Increments for each visible pass and is shown on every 
screen; final total printed at the end.

Real Life Examples:

Three UK TV Satellite's

All three are  geostationary, they appear fixed in the sky. 
The approximate look angles are given for  Manchester  (53.5°N, 2.2°W)
at  20:30 UTC  on any given day. 
For a perfect geostationary orbit, set:

-  Position X  = 42164 km (geostationary radius)  
-  Position Y  = 0 km  
-  Position Z  = 0 km  
-  Velocity X  = 0 km/s  
-  Velocity Y  = 3.0747 km/s (circular speed)  
-  Velocity Z  = 0 km/s  

| Satellite Name   | Orbital Longitude  | Compass Bearing | Elevation | Typical TV Services         |
|------------------|--------------------|-----------------|-----------|-----------------------------|
|Astra 1  (19.2°E) | 19.2°E | ~165°(SSE)|  ~26°           |           |European channels            |
|Astra 2  (28.2°E) | 28.2°E | ~155°(SSE)|  ~22°           |           |BBC,ITV,C4,Sky UK(main UK TV)|
|Eutelsat Hot Bird | (13°E) |  13.0°E   |  ~172°S         |~30°       |UK-targeted channels+European|


Notes on the Values:

Compass bearing  is measured clockwise from true north. 
A value of 165° means the satellite is seen in the south-southeast.

Elevation  is the angle above the horizon, 
a higher number means the satellite is higher in the sky.

These values are  approximate; exact angles depend on the
observer’s exact location and the satellite’s precise orbital
inclination (usually very small for geostationary satellites).

To simulate any of these satellites, simply enter the 
corresponding initial position and velocity when prompted.
 
The BASIC program will then show passes every 15 minutes 
throughout the 24-hour period (the satellite will remain fixed, 
so you will see 96 passes). 

The displayed altitude, velocity, and acceleration will be correct
and the compass/elevation will match the values above.
